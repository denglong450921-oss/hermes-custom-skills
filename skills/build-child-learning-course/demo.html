Concept proposal · Ages 6–8

The Lantern Garden

A gentle number-sense adventure where children become junior lightkeepers, waking a sleepy garden by building tiny lantern creatures from number pairs.

Number bonds to 10 8–12 minute sessions Solo + family share Concept stage
Invitation One mystery. One obvious move.
Replay The garden changes because the child did.
!
The fun promise

Pair glowing numbers, invent a lantern creature, and watch your garden wake up.

Learner and context

Design for this child, not “children.”

The concept assumes an early primary learner who understands counting but still relies on objects or fingers to compose ten. Reading demand stays low; strategy and confidence matter more than speed.

Learner Ages 6–8, emerging independent readers
Learning goal Compose and decompose numbers to 10
Play context Short solo play with a shareable creation
Success evidence Build, explain, and transfer a number pair
Reference design enhancement

Keep the reason. Improve the expression.

The reference contributes useful intent and mechanisms. Its surface choices are adapted only when they strengthen this learner’s sense of agency, competence, curiosity, and safety.

Reference signal Decision Enhanced direction Why it fits better
Begin without long instructions Retain The first number pair is completed through a playable demonstration. Success teaches the rule faster than explanation.
Stars and world restoration Adapt Correct strategies create unique lantern creatures and visible garden life. Progress expresses competence instead of becoming a detached currency.
Always-present companion Replace A quiet moth guide appears only after hesitation or a request for help. The child keeps authorship; support remains available without interruption.
Reward-focused success criteria Add Track strategy change, emotional recovery, explanation, and transfer. Enjoyment, comfort, and learning need separate evidence.
Recommended concept

Learning is the move that changes the world.

Children do not answer a worksheet and then receive entertainment. Pairing, composing, and explaining numbers are the actions that grow the garden.

Fantasy and role

Junior lightkeeper

Night has arrived early. Each lantern creature needs two number-glows that combine to its target brightness. The child discovers which pairs make it wake.

Core game verb Pair → build → explain

Choose two glows, combine them, then show how the total was made.

Meaningful choice Several pairs can work

The child chooses a valid strategy and a creature form, not one obvious button.

Game consequence The garden remembers

Different number strategies produce different plants, light patterns, and paths.

Learning evidence Production plus transfer

The child constructs a pair, explains it, and uses it in a new garden problem.

Core loop

Five beats. One satisfying thought.

The shortest loop is clear enough to enter quickly and rich enough to support different strategies. Feedback names what happened; it never replaces thinking.

3

4

Acceptance and enjoyment

Invite. Support. Then step back.

Enjoyment grows from competence, autonomy, curiosity, and meaning—not pressure, spectacle, or an endless prize economy.

01

Playable first turn

A moth silently demonstrates one move, then pauses so the child completes it.

02

Earned early success

The first target allows several familiar pairs, so success is genuine but likely.

03

Safe mistakes

A dim lantern reveals the mismatch and returns the pieces without loss or shame.

04

Bounded agency

Children choose a strategy, creature form, and garden placement within a clear goal.

05

Meaningful growth

The garden’s paths record explored number relationships rather than points collected.

06

Quiet accessibility

No essential timer; color has shape partners; speech, touch, and keyboard have alternatives.

Progression and replay

Keep the language. Deepen the thinking.

The interaction stays familiar while support, representation, and transfer distance change one main dimension at a time.

Welcome Copy a demonstrated pair with objects still visible.
Explore Choose among several valid pairs and compare outcomes.
Master Build from a target with fewer visual supports.
Transfer Use number pairs to solve a new path or sharing problem.
Revisit Invent a creature whose glow can be made two ways.
Concept validation

Observe four truths separately.

A child can enjoy a game without learning, learn without wanting to return, or persist while feeling uncomfortable. The concept should not collapse these signals into one score.

Acceptance

Will the child begin?

  • Understands the first action without a long explanation
  • Reaches an independent move quickly
  • Knows what changed after the move
Enjoyment

Is the child choosing to continue?

  • Shows curiosity about another pair or creature
  • Changes strategy rather than repeating blindly
  • Requests another round without reward pressure
Learning

Can the child use the idea?

  • Constructs a valid number pair independently
  • Explains the relationship in words or objects
  • Transfers it to a new representation
Comfort

Can the child recover safely?

  • Continues after a mismatch without embarrassment
  • Uses help without losing ownership
  • Experiences no avoidable sensory or motor barrier
